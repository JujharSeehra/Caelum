CAELUM

Computational Modelling and Optimization of NaOH/Ca(OH)₂ Carbon Capture

Research Results, April - October 2026

Overview Results Methodology Machine Learning Conclusion

Project Overview

Caelum is a computational research project investigating a hybrid sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) chemical-looping system for carbon dioxide capture.

The study evaluates two applications: direct air capture (DAC), using atmospheric CO₂ concentrations of approximately 420 ppm, and industrial flue-gas capture, using an inlet CO₂ concentration of 12%.

A Python-based physics and technoeconomic model was used to simulate absorber performance, chemical regeneration, energy requirements, and capture costs. Machine-learning regression and numerical optimization were also evaluated.

Key Findings

Industrial capture achieved higher overall capture efficiency and lower modelled capture cost than direct air capture. The industrial configuration captured approximately 17.4 times more CO₂ annually.

The estimated cost per tonne for DAC was approximately 10.3 times the industrial capture cost. However, the optimized configurations used different operating conditions and should not be interpreted as a comparison of equally sized facilities.

Electricity represented approximately 63.6% of annual modelled DAC cost, while Ca(OH)₂ consumption accounted for approximately 83.0% of industrial annual cost.

Methodology

The simulation incorporates gas-liquid CO₂ absorption, causticization, flow-dependent energy requirements, and annualized economic costs.

Chemical Reactions

CO₂ absorption:

2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O

Causticization:

Na₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃ + 2NaOH

Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide, producing sodium carbonate. Calcium hydroxide then regenerates sodium hydroxide while producing calcium carbonate.

Simulation and Optimization

Nine process variables were varied, including absorber dimensions, gas and liquid flow rates, NaOH concentration, causticizer volume, number of causticization stages, reaction-rate parameters, and equilibrium conversion.

Differential evolution was used to minimize modelled capture cost while maintaining a minimum overall capture efficiency of 70% for DAC and 90% for industrial capture. Candidate configurations were subsequently evaluated using the full process model.

Machine-Learning Results

Random Forest regression models were trained to predict capture efficiency and levelized capture cost. Separate models were developed for DAC and industrial capture.

Each model used 150 decision trees, with 80% of the simulated configurations used for training and 20% reserved for testing.

Conclusion

The Caelum simulations indicate that NaOH/Ca(OH)₂ chemical looping can achieve substantial modelled CO₂ capture in both industrial and atmospheric applications.

Within the investigated design space, industrial capture demonstrated more favourable capture efficiency and estimated cost than DAC. DAC performance was strongly affected by electrical energy demand, particularly blower operation.

The study provides a computational basis for further investigation of absorber design, chemical regeneration, energy efficiency, and technoeconomic optimization.

Research Limitations

The results depend on simplified engineering correlations, assumed economic parameters, and simulated operating conditions. Experimental validation, refined equipment costing, and additional sensitivity analysis are needed before claims about commercial feasibility can be made.

The reported configurations are the best verified solutions identified within the investigated search space, not mathematically proven global optima.
